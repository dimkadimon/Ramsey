\author{Dmitry Kamenetsky}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Let $N(c,d)$ denote the least $R$ such that every $c$-colouring of the grid $[R]^d$
contains a monochromatic \emph{box}, i.e.\ a set
$\{x_1,x_1'\}\times\cdots\times\{x_d,x_d'\}$ with $x_i\neq x_i'$ for every $i$, all of
whose $2^d$ points share one colour. These numbers --- the cube case of the grid Ramsey
problem for boxes --- satisfy $N(c,d)=\Theta(c^{2^d})$ with the exponent known only up to
a factor of $d$, and almost nothing is known about their exact values.
This note is a computational study of the case $d=3$, $c\le5$.

Our main contribution is computational: \emph{explicit} box-free colourings of $[n]^3$
with $n=23,40,44$ for $c=3,4,5$, each verified by exhaustive search over all
$\binom{n}{2}^3$ boxes, which give $N(3,3)\ge24$, $N(4,3)\ge41$, $N(5,3)\ge45$.
Cooper--Fenner--Purewal give the matching upper bounds $93,277,657$ for these $c$ but
tabulate no cube colourings; we found no lower bounds for $c\ge3$ in the literature.

For $c=2$ we sharpen the upper bound. Writing $\varphi_c(n)$ for the least number of
monochromatic rectangles in a $c$-colouring of $[n]^2$, the number of features that a
slice must use gives the necessary condition $n\varphi_c(n)\le c\binom{n}{2}^{2}$
(Lemma~15 of Cooper--Fenner--Purewal in packing language). Two exact levels of convexity
give $\varphi_2(21)\ge4205$, so a box-free $2$-colouring of $[21]^3$ would need
$21\cdot4205=88305$ features out of the $88200$ available; hence $N(2,3)\le21$,
improving the bound $22$ obtained from their double-counting recursion. The wall at
$n=21$ is the projective plane of order $4$: at $n=21$ the continuous form of the argument
is exactly tight, and the improvement is purely an integrality effect
(Remark~\ref{rem:pp}).

On the structural side we give a layer (feature-packing) formulation, an exact criterion
for shift-orbit families, a dimension-raising lemma, and an exact reduction of the
three-dimensional problem, for colourings constant on the level sets of $x+y$, to a
two-dimensional \emph{corner} condition (Section~\ref{sec:1dshift}). We are explicit
throughout about which statements are re-derivations of known results. Code, colourings
and verification scripts are available at
\url{https://github.com/dimkadimon/Ramsey}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem}
For integers $n,d\ge 1$ let $[n]^d=\{1,\dots,n\}^d$. A \emph{$d$-box} is a set
\[
B=\{x_1,y_1\}\times\cdots\times\{x_d,y_d\}\subseteq[n]^d,
\qquad x_i\neq y_i \text{ for all } i .
\]
(These are the ``combinatorial boxes''; geometrically they are the vertex sets of
axis-parallel rectangular boxes, i.e.\ of prisms when $d=3$.) A colouring
$f\colon[n]^d\to[c]$ is \emph{box-free}, and $[n]^d$ is \emph{$c$-colourable}, if some
colour class restriction has no monochromatic box; otherwise $[n]^d$ is
\emph{$c$-guaranteed}. Finally
\[
\Ncd=\min\{R : [R]^d \text{ is $c$-guaranteed}\}.
\]
We write $\Ncd[3]$ for the three-dimensional case, which is the subject of this note.
Note that $c$-guaranteedness is monotone: if $[n]^d$ is $c$-guaranteed then so is
$[n']^d$ for $n'\ge n$, and a box-free colouring of $[n']^d$ restricts to one of
$[n]^d$ for $n\le n'$. The latter is used constantly below.

The problem is the cube case of the grid Ramsey problem for boxes. The general study is
due to Cooper, Fenner and Purewal \cite{CFP}, who determine the order of growth of the
largest volume of a $c$-colourable grid, bound the volume of minimal obstructions, and
analyse the case $c=2,d=3$ computationally; closely related estimates for equilateral
grids go back to Agnarsson, Doerr and Schoen \cite{ADS}. The problem also appears as the
Ramsey problem for hyperoctahedra $K_d(2)$ in complete $d$-partite $d$-uniform
hypergraphs, and relaxing van der Waerden's theorem to boxes is one motivation
\cite{CFP,FGP}.

For $d=1$ the answer is immediate: $[n]$ is $c$-guaranteed iff $n\ge c+1$, so
$N(c,1)=c+1$. For general $d$ the known bounds are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:known}
2^{\,2^d/(2d)} \;\lesssim\; \Ncd \;\lesssim\; (d+2)\,2^{\,2^{d-1}},
\end{equation}
see \cite[Thm.~2]{CFP} for the lower bound and \cite[Cor.~3.6]{CFP} for the upper bound;
both are proved by counting monochromatic boxes. The exponent is thus known only up to a
factor $\Theta(d)$, and closing this gap appears to be open.

\subsection{What this note does, and what it does not}
This note is an extended computational and structural study of the case $d=3$ with
$c\le 5$. Our contribution is deliberately stated in two parts.

\emph{What is (as far as we can determine) not in the literature.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Lower bounds for $c\ge3$.} Explicit box-free colourings of $[n]^3$ for
  $c=3,4,5$ with $n=23,40,44$, each verified exhaustively
  (Section~\ref{sec:construct}); these give $N(3,3)\ge 24$, $N(4,3)\ge41$,
  $N(5,3)\ge45$. \cite{CFP} tabulates no cube colourings for $c\ge3$, and we found no
  lower bounds for these parameters elsewhere. These are the most substantial new
  numerical statements in this note.
\item A layer formulation (Proposition~\ref{prop:packing}) that converts the problem into
  a packing problem for monochromatic rectangles, together with measurements of how much
  of the ``feature space'' optimal colourings actually use (Table~\ref{tab:features}).
\item An exact criterion for shift-orbit constructions (Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}),
  including the role of rectangles fixed by a non-trivial shift; this yields a proof that
  the single-coordinate family fails for all $n\ge12$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sidon}) and
  explains the empirically observed barrier at $n=12$.
\item A dimension-raising lemma (Theorem~\ref{thm:raise}) giving
  $N(2,d+1)\le N(2,d)$, hence $N(2,d)\ge 12$ for all $d\ge3$, constructively.
\item \textbf{An exact one-dimensional-shift reduction} (Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift}):
  for colourings of the form $\chi(x,y,z)=f(x+y,z)$ the three-dimensional condition is
  \emph{equivalent} to a family of two-dimensional corner conditions on the level sets of
  $x+y$, which replaces $n^3$ free bits by $2n^2$ and puts the sum-type algebraic
  colourings into a common framework.
\item \textbf{An improved upper bound for the two-colour cube}: $N(2,3)\le21$
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2}), against the value $22$ obtained from Lemma~4 of
  \cite{CFP}. The packing condition $n\varphi_c(n)\le c\binom{n}{2}^2$ is Lemma~15 of
  \cite{CFP}; what is new is bounding $\varphi_2(21)$ by two exact levels of convexity
  and using the integrality of the row composition, which is what makes $n=21$ fail
  (Remark~\ref{rem:pp}). The same computation gives nothing new for $c\ge3$.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{What is not new, and is stated here for completeness and comparison.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The Zarankiewicz bound $\Ncd[3]\le 22,93,277,657$ ($c=2,3,4,5$) coincides
  \emph{exactly} with the values produced by Lemma~4 of \cite{CFP} applied to the cube.
  Our proof (Section~\ref{sec:upper}) is structurally different --- a Zarankiewicz/$C_4$
  argument --- but it is not stronger, and we make no priority claim. Only the $c=2$
  value is improved, and only by one, in Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2}.
\item Our $c=2$ lower bound $N(2,3)\ge 12$ is weaker than the bound $N(2,3)\ge13$ already
  implied by \cite[Fig.~3]{CFP}: that table asserts that $[12,12,27]$ is 2-colourable,
  and such a colouring restricts to $[12]^3$.
\item The random-colouring lower bound of Section~\ref{sec:growth} is the standard
  Lov\'asz-local-lemma/first-moment argument of \cite{CFP,ADS}.
\end{enumerate}
Accordingly the note should be read as an experimental-mathematics contribution: the
numerical content is the lower bounds for $c=3,4,5$ (which are new) together with a
one-unit improvement of the $c=2$ upper bound, and the structural content is the
collection of criteria, each of which converts a three-dimensional statement into a
lower-dimensional one and thereby identifies where the difficulty lies.

\section{A layer formulation}\label{sec:layer}

\subsection{Features}
Fix $c$ and $n$. For $x\in[n]$ let $f_x$ denote the $x$-th \emph{slice} of a colouring
$f\colon[n]^3\to[c]$, i.e.\ the map $(y,z)\mapsto f(x,y,z)$. A rectangle is a set
$R=\{y_1,y_2\}\times\{z_1,z_2\}\subseteq[n]^2$ with $y_1\neq y_2$, $z_1\neq z_2$, and it
is \emph{$\gamma$-monochromatic} for $f_x$ if $f_x$ is constantly $\gamma$ on $R$.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{feature} is a pair $(\gamma,R)$ where $\gamma\in[c]$ and $R$ is a rectangle.
A slice $f_x$ \emph{uses} $(\gamma,R)$ if $R$ is $\gamma$-monochromatic for $f_x$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Packing criterion]\label{prop:packing}
$f\colon[n]^3\to[c]$ has a monochromatic box if and only if some feature is used by two
distinct slices. Consequently $[n]^3$ is $c$-colourable if and only if there are $n$
colourings $f_1,\dots,f_n$ of $[n]^2$ whose used-feature sets are pairwise disjoint.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A box $\{x_1,x_2\}\times\{y_1,y_2\}\times\{z_1,z_2\}$ is monochromatic in colour $\gamma$
exactly when both slices $f_{x_1}$ and $f_{x_2}$ are $\gamma$-monochromatic on the
rectangle $R=\{y_1,y_2\}\times\{z_1,z_2\}$, i.e.\ when $(\gamma,R)$ is used by both.
\end{proof}

There are $F_c(n)=c\binom{n}{2}^2$ features in total, and Proposition~\ref{prop:packing}
turns the problem into a set-packing problem. Its value in practice is that the packing
condition is checkable in $O(c\,n^4)$ time and that each slice is a bounded,
SAT-checkable object.

\begin{corollary}[Counting necessary condition]\label{cor:count}
If $[n]^3$ is $c$-colourable and every $c$-colouring of $[n]^2$ uses at least
$\nu_c(n)$ features, then $n\,\nu_c(n)\le F_c(n)=c\binom{n}{2}^2$.
\end{corollary}
For $c=2$ and $n=12$ this reads $\nu_2(12)\le 726$, and $\nu_2(12)\approx424$ in practice,
so the counting condition is far from tight: the obstruction is geometric, not numerical.

\section{Constructions}\label{sec:construct}

\subsection{Algorithm}
Slices are built one at a time. Given the multiset of features already used by slices
$1,\dots,x-1$, we ask a SAT solver for \emph{any} $c$-colouring of $[n]^2$ that uses none
of them (each feature is a four-literal clause), and then improve that slice by a
bit-mask local search which reduces its number of monochromatic rectangles without
creating a forbidden feature. The improved slice is committed, its features are added to
the forbidden set, and the process repeats. If a slice cannot be found we backtrack or
restart. All colourings reported were then verified independently.

\section{Structural limits of shift-orbit constructions}\label{sec:shift}

A natural attempt is to take one base colouring $f$ of $[n]^2$ and let the slices be the
translates of $f$ along a cyclic group. Write $v=(a,b)\in(\mathbb{Z}_n)^2$ for a shift
vector of order $n$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shift}
f_x(y,z)=f\big((y-xa)\bmod n,\;(z-xb)\bmod n\big).
\end{equation}
The single-coordinate family is $v=(0,1)$; the diagonal family is $v=(1,1)$; all other
slopes are interpolations.

\begin{theorem}[Exact criterion]\label{thm:criterion}
The colouring \eqref{eq:shift} is box-free if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item no rectangle that is fixed by a non-zero element of $\langle v\rangle$ is
  monochromatic in $f$, and
\item among the rectangles not fixed by any non-zero element of $\langle v\rangle$, at
  most one per $\langle v\rangle$-orbit is monochromatic in $f$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A box over slices $x_1<x_2$ is monochromatic in colour $\gamma$ exactly when the
rectangle $R_1=R-x_1v$ and $R_2=R-x_2v$ are both $\gamma$-monochromatic in $f$, where
$R$ is the projected rectangle. Thus we need: for every orbit $\mathcal{O}$ and every
$\gamma$, the number of $\gamma$-monochromatic rectangles in $\mathcal{O}$ is at most
the number of slices in which a given rectangle appears. A rectangle $R$ appears in
slice $x$ whenever $R-xv$ is monochromatic, i.e.\ whenever some translate of $R$ is
monochromatic, so a rectangle appears in as many slices as the size of its orbit. If the
orbit has size $n$ exactly one monochromatic rectangle is allowed; if the orbit is
smaller (equivalently $R$ is fixed by some non-zero shift) each element of the orbit
appears in more than one slice and no monochromatic rectangle at all is allowed.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Condition (a) is the non-obvious half. For even $n$ and $v=(1,1)$, the shift $t=n/2$
acts on the coordinates as $y\mapsto y+n/2$, $z\mapsto z+n/2$, which maps a rectangle
with both side lengths exactly $n/2$ to itself. Hence for $n=12$ a base colouring
satisfying \eqref{eq:shift} must contain \emph{no} monochromatic rectangle spanning
exactly $6$ rows or exactly $6$ columns. Ignoring this condition produces invalid
colourings; we mention it because an early version of our local search did exactly that.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The single-coordinate family]\label{prop:sidon}
For $v=(0,1)$ the criterion of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} is equivalent to: for every
colour $\gamma$ and every pair of columns $\{z_1,z_2\}$, the set
$S(\gamma,\{z_1,z_2\})=\{y : f(y,z_1)=f(y,z_2)=\gamma\}$ is a Sidon set in
$\mathbb{Z}_n$ (all pairwise differences distinct up to sign, and --- for even $n$ ---
with no antipodal pair). Consequently this family gives no box-free 2-colouring of
$[n]^3$ for any $n\ge 12$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $d=x_2-x_1$ and rows $a=y_1-x_1$, $b=y_2-x_1$, a monochromatic box requires
$a,b,a-d,b-d\in S$. Two two-element sets are translates of one another exactly when they
have the same oriented difference, so a repeat of any difference produces a box. The
Sidon condition gives $|S|(|S|-1)\le n-1$; a balanced 2-colouring has $|S|\approx n/4$ on
average, so $n^2/16\lesssim n$ is needed, which fails for $n\ge 12$ (and a SAT check
confirms unsatisfiability at $n=12$ in under three minutes).
\end{proof}

For the remaining slopes we searched the orbit objective of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}
by weighted breakout local search over all shift vectors of order $n$ (96 of them at $n=12$). At
$n=11$ solutions are found immediately; at $n=12$ every slope stalls with at least nine
violated orbits. Combined with Proposition~\ref{prop:sidon}, this is evidence --- not a
proof --- that no shift-orbit construction gives a box-free 2-colouring of $[12]^3$.

\begin{theorem}[Dimension raising]\label{thm:raise}
If $f$ is a box-free 2-colouring of $[n]^d$, then
\[
F(x_1,\dots,x_{d+1})=f\big((x_1-x_{d+1})\bmod n,\dots,(x_d-x_{d+1})\bmod n\big)
\]
is a box-free 2-colouring of $[n]^{d+1}$. Hence $N(2,d+1)\le N(2,d)$, and $N(2,d)\ge12$
for every $d\ge3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $B$ be a box in $[n]^{d+1}$ and split its $2^{d+1}$ points by the last coordinate
into two $d$-boxes $B_u$, $B_v$ (the same $d$-box at the two values of $x_{d+1}$). If $B$
is monochromatic then $B_u$ and $B_v$ are monochromatic in the same colour. But
$F(\cdot,u)$ and $F(\cdot,v)$ are $f$ shifted by $u$ resp.\ $v$ in every coordinate, and
being monochromatic is invariant under a shift of all coordinates, so $B_u$ being
monochromatic already means $f$ has a monochromatic $d$-box, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The lift was verified exhaustively at $d=4$: all $\binom{11}{2}^4=9\,150\,625$ four-boxes
of the lift of the colouring \eqref{eq:qr} were enumerated, none monochromatic. A
sensitivity check (forcing a monochromatic $2^4$ sub-block) makes the checker report
exactly one box, so the check is not vacuous.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Reduction to one-dimensional shifts}\label{sec:1dshift}

The colourings of Section~\ref{sec:construct} are produced by search, not by a formula,
and the one algebraic colouring we know explicitly (the quadratic-residue colouring of
$n=11$, Appendix~\ref{app:colourings}) is of \emph{sum type}: its colour is a function of
$x+y+z$. Motivated by that we consider the family of colourings that are constant on the
level sets of $x+y$, i.e.\ $\chi(x,y,z)=f(x+y,z)$, and show that within this family the
three-dimensional box condition collapses to a two-dimensional one.

Fix $n$ and a function $f$ on the set below, and set
$\chi_f(x,y,z)=f(x+y,z)$ for $(x,y,z)\in[n]^3$. For a colour $\gamma$ and two levels
$z_1\neq z_2$ put
\[
D_\gamma(z_1,z_2)\;=\;\bigl\{u\in[0,2n-2]\;:\;f(u,z_1)=f(u,z_2)=\gamma\bigr\}, \qquad
f\colon[0,2n-2]\times[n]\to\{0,1\}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Shift criterion]\label{prop:1dshift}
$\chi_f$ is box-free if and only if for every colour $\gamma$, every $z_1<z_2$, every
$1\le a,b\le n-1$ and every $u_0\in[0,\,2n-2-a-b]$, the four values
$u_0,\;u_0+a,\;u_0+b,\;u_0+a+b$ are \emph{not} all contained in $D_\gamma(z_1,z_2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x_1<x_2$, $y_1<y_2$, $z_1<z_2$ and put $b=x_2-x_1\ge1$, $a=y_2-y_1\ge1$ and
$u_0=x_1+y_1$. The first coordinate $x+y$ of the eight vertices of the box takes exactly
the values $u_0,\,u_0+a,\,u_0+b,\,u_0+a+b$, and the third coordinate takes the two values
$z_1,z_2$. Hence the eight vertices all have colour $\gamma$ if and only if those four
values all lie in $D_\gamma(z_1,z_2)$. (Values may coincide among the four; then some of
the eight vertices coincide, but the box is still a box and the four values are still
required to lie in $D_\gamma$.)
The bounds $a,b\le n-1$ are exactly the range of the differences $y_2-y_1$ and $x_2-x_1$.
Conversely, let $\gamma$, $z_1<z_2$, $1\le a,b\le n-1$ and $u_0\in[0,2n-2-a-b]$ be such
that the four values lie in $D_\gamma(z_1,z_2)$. Since $x_1$ ranges over $[0,n-1-b]$ and
$y_1$ over $[0,n-1-a]$, the sum $x_1+y_1$ ranges over all of $[0,\,2n-2-a-b]$; choose
$x_1,y_1$ with $x_1+y_1=u_0$ and put $x_2=x_1+b$, $y_2=y_1+a$. The eight points then lie in
$[n]^3$ and all have colour $\gamma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:ap}
For every $z_1<z_2$ and colour $\gamma$ the set $D_\gamma(z_1,z_2)\subseteq[2,2n]$ is free
of three-term arithmetic progressions (take $a=b$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift}) and
free of corners $\{u_0,u_0+a,u_0+b,u_0+a+b\}$ with $a,b\ge1$.
\end{corollary}

Two consequences are worth recording. First, the sum-type colouring
$\chi(x,y,z)=g(x+y+z)$ is the special case $f(u,z)=g(u+z)$ (indices reduced modulo $n$),
so Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift} is the natural common framework for the algebraic
colourings. Second, the proposition replaces the $n^3$ free bits of a general colouring by
the $2n^2$ bits of $f$, and turns each slice pair into an independent corner-freeness
condition on a finite set --- a shape that local search can handle far better than the
original constraint. We exploited this in the searches reported in
Section~\ref{sec:1dsearch} and we stress that the reduction is \emph{exact}: it neither
loses nor gains colourings, so a box-free colouring found in this family is a genuine one.

\begin{remark}
Corollary~\ref{cor:ap} should not be read as an impossibility result. It is tempting to
conclude that the family dies at $n=9$ because a two-colouring of $[n]$ always contains a
monochromatic three-term progression ($W(2,3)=9$); but $D_0$ and $D_1$ need not cover
$[2,2n]$ (a value $u$ with $f(u,z_1)\neq f(u,z_2)$ belongs to neither), so no such
conclusion follows. We record this because an earlier version of our notes contained the
erroneous claim that the family is empty for $n\ge9$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Search in the shift family}\label{sec:1dsearch}

Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift} is a constraint satisfaction problem in $2n^2$ bits
($2n-1$ values of $u$ times $n$ values of $z$), with one forbidden configuration per
colour, per pair $z_1<z_2$, per $1\le a,b\le n-1$ and per admissible $u_0$. Each
forbidden configuration is a clause over eight variables, so the instance has $O(n^5)$
clauses of width eight and only $2n^2$ variables; for $n=14$ this is $378$ variables and
$399\,854$ clauses, against $14^3=2744$ variables and $1.5\times10^6$ clauses for the
direct encoding of the original problem. We solved these instances with a CDCL solver
(CaDiCaL, via \texttt{python-sat}).

Two checks fix the meaning of the results. First, the sum-type colouring
$\chi(x,y,z)=g(x+y+z)$ is the case $f(u,z)=g(u+z)$; for $n=11$ with $g$ the quadratic
residues mod $11$ the criterion evaluates to zero violations, and the solver re-finds a
box-free colouring of $[11]^3$ inside the family in $53$~s. Second, every colouring
returned is re-verified from the definition by an independent checker
(\texttt{core.is\_valid\_fast}), which scans all $\binom{n}{2}^3$ boxes. The family
therefore neither loses nor manufactures colourings, as claimed in
Section~\ref{sec:1dshift}.

\begin{remark}
The bound $b\le n-1$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift} is not cosmetic. An earlier
version of the criterion imposed only $a,b\ge1$, which is \emph{strictly stronger}: for
$n=11$ it assigns $360$ violations to the genuine colouring of
Appendix~\ref{app:colourings} and thereby rejects it, because it admits configurations
such as $a=1$, $b=11$ whose ``difference in $x$'' exceeds the width of the grid. We
mention this because the failure is silent --- the criterion remains sufficient, so a
search based on it returns only valid colourings and the error is invisible unless one
tests it against a known example.
\end{remark}

For local search the useful identity is the following. For a set $D$ of positions and
integers $a,b\ge1$, the number of violating configurations with those $a,b$ is
$|D\cap(D-a)\cap(D-b)\cap(D-a-b)|$, and summing over \emph{all} $b\ge1$ gives
$\binom{|D\cap(D-a)|}{2}$, so the unbounded violation count is computable with $O(n)$
bit operations. With the truncation $b\le n-1$ a direct double loop over $(a,b)$ is
needed, but each $D$ is then a bitmask and the whole cost of a state is a few thousand
popcount operations.

\section{Upper bounds}\label{sec:upper}

\subsection{A Zarankiewicz argument}
The following is elementary and self-contained.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:upper}
Let $c\ge2$, $n\ge2$ and suppose that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:criterion}
n^2\binom{n/c}{2}\;>\;\binom{n}{2}\,Z_2(n),
\qquad Z_2(n):=\tfrac{n}{2}+\tfrac{n}{2}\sqrt{4n-3}.
\end{equation}
Then $[n]^3$ is $c$-guaranteed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $f\colon[n]^3\to[c]$ be box-free. For a colour $\gamma$ and two slices $x_1<x_2$ put
$B_\gamma(x_1,x_2)=\{(y,z): f(x_1,y,z)=f(x_2,y,z)=\gamma\}\subseteq[n]^2$. If
$B_\gamma(x_1,x_2)$ contained a rectangle, its eight points together with $x_1,x_2$ would
form a monochromatic box; so $B_\gamma(x_1,x_2)$ is $C_4$-free as a bipartite graph and
therefore has at most $Z_2(n)$ elements, since a $C_4$-free bipartite graph with parts of
size $n$ and $e$ edges satisfies $\sum_y\binom{d_y}{2}\le\binom{n}{2}$ and, by convexity,
$\sum_y\binom{d_y}{2}\ge (e^2/n-e)/2$.

Write $m_\gamma(y,z)=\#\{x: f(x,y,z)=\gamma\}$. Double counting gives
$\sum_{x_1<x_2}|B_\gamma(x_1,x_2)|=\sum_{y,z}\binom{m_\gamma(y,z)}{2}$. Since
$\sum_\gamma m_\gamma(y,z)=n$ and $t\mapsto\binom{t}{2}$ is convex,
$\sum_\gamma\binom{m_\gamma(y,z)}{2}\ge c\binom{n/c}{2}$ for every $(y,z)$. Hence
\[
n^2\,c\binom{n/c}{2}\;\le\;\sum_\gamma\sum_{x_1<x_2}|B_\gamma(x_1,x_2)|
\;\le\;\binom{n}{2}\,c\,Z_2(n). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

Numerically \eqref{eq:criterion} first fails at $n=22$ for $c=2$, giving
$\Ncd[3]\le22$; the same computation gives $93$, $277$, $657$, $1341$ for
$c=3,4,5,6$.

\subsection{A packing bound and an improvement for $c=2$}\label{sec:packbound}

In the feature language of Section~\ref{sec:layer}, Lemma~15 of \cite{CFP} says that
the $n$ slices of a box-free colouring must use \emph{pairwise disjoint} features. Since a
slice uses exactly as many features as it has monochromatic rectangles,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pack}
n\,\varphi_c(n)\;\le\;c\binom{n}{2}^{2}
\end{equation}
is necessary for a box-free $c$-colouring of $[n]^3$ to exist, where $\varphi_c(n)$ is the
least number of monochromatic rectangles in a $c$-colouring of $[n]^{2}$.
Cooper--Fenner--Purewal bound $\varphi_c$ through their recursion. For $c=2$ we bound
$\varphi_2(n)$ by two exact levels of convexity, and the integrality of the intermediate
composition decides $n=21$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:upper2}
$\varphi_2(21)\ge 4205$. Consequently $[21]^3$ is $2$-guaranteed, i.e.\ $\Ncd[3]\le21$
for $c=2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $k_y$ be the number of $0$-cells in row $y$ of a $2$-colouring of $[21]^2$. For each
of the $\binom{21}{2}=210$ column pairs $j$ let $a_j$ (resp.\ $b_j$) be the number of rows
whose two cells in $j$ are both $0$ (resp.\ both $1$). The number of monochromatic
rectangles is
\[
R=\sum_j\Bigl[\binom{a_j}{2}+\binom{b_j}{2}\Bigr],
\qquad
\sum_j a_j=T_0:=\sum_y\binom{k_y}{2},\quad
\sum_j b_j=T_1:=\sum_y\binom{21-k_y}{2}.
\]
For integers $x_j\ge0$ with $\sum_j x_j=T$ convexity gives
$\sum_j\binom{x_j}{2}\ge\mu(T,210)$, where $\mu$ is attained by distributing $T$ as evenly
as possible over the $210$ bins; hence $R\ge\mu(T_0,210)+\mu(T_1,210)$. Each row
contributes $\binom{k}{2}+\binom{21-k}{2}\ge\binom{10}{2}+\binom{11}{2}=100$, an even
number. Therefore either $T_0+T_1=2100$, in which case every row has $k_y\in\{10,11\}$
and $(T_0,T_1)=(945+10m,\,1155-10m)$ for some integer $0\le m\le21$; or $T_0+T_1\ge2102$.
In the first case $\mu(T_0,210)+\mu(T_1,210)$ is minimised at $m=10,11$, where it equals
$\mu(1045,210)+\mu(1055,210)=2080+2125=4205$; in the second it is at least
$2\mu(1051,210)=4210$. Hence $R\ge4205$.
Finally \eqref{eq:pack} at $c=2$, $n=21$ would require
$21\cdot4205=88305\le2\binom{21}{2}^{2}=88200$, which fails.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:pp}
The obstruction is a projective plane. The continuous form of the argument ($T_0=T_1=1050$)
gives only $\varphi_2(21)\ge4200$ and $21\cdot4200=88200$, \emph{exactly} the number of
features available; $21=4^2+4+1$ is a projective-plane order and the extremal Zarankiewicz
value $Z_2(21)=105=5\cdot21$ is attained by the plane of order $4$, which is also the
extremal example for the sharpness of the K\H{o}vari--S\'os--Tur\'an bound in the case
$t=2$. So no refinement of the continuous count can pass $n=21$: the improvement above
comes precisely from the fact that $T_0=T_1=1050$ is not attainable by an integer row
composition, and the least integer excess costs $105$ features.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Applying the same computation with $c$ colours, \eqref{eq:pack} first fails at
$n=93,277,657$ for $c=3,4,5$ --- the same thresholds as Lemma~4 of \cite{CFP}, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2} is an improvement only for $c=2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relation to the literature}
The Zarankiewicz Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} is \emph{not} an improvement: it reproduces
Lemma~4 of \cite{CFP} applied to the cube (the values $22,93,277,657$ for $c=2,3,4,5$).
Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2} improves the $c=2$ value from $22$ to $21$; we found no
post-2008 improvement of the cube bounds in the literature, and \cite{CFP} itself remarks
that ``the case of $c=2$ and $d=3$'' is where the interesting computational questions
remain open.

The $c=2$ lower bound deserves a word. Our own best construction gives $[11]^3$, i.e.\

\[

\]
The lower bound is not due to us. The upper bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2}; the

\section{The growth of \texorpdfstring{$N(2,d)$}{N(2,d)}}\label{sec:growth}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:random}
For every $d\ge2$ and every $n$ with $\binom{n}{2}^{d}<2^{\,2^d-1}$ there is a box-free
2-colouring of $[n]^d$. In particular
\[
N(2,d)\;>\;2^{\,2^d/(2d)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Colour the $n^d$ points independently and uniformly. A fixed box is monochromatic in
either colour with probability $2^{1-2^d}$, so the union bound gives
$\Pr[\exists\text{ monochromatic box}]\le\binom{n}{2}^d 2^{1-2^d}<1$. Using
$\binom{n}{2}\ge n^2/4$ yields $n^{2d}/4^d<2^{2^d-1}$, i.e.\
$n<2^{(2^d-1)/(2d)+1}$, which implies the claim for $d\ge2$.
\end{proof}

The question whether $N(2,d)\ge 2^{c\,2^d}$ holds for a \emph{fixed} $c>0$ is open; it
would amount to improving the exponent by a factor $d$. Four independent counting
arguments all produce the same $2^d/d$ rate:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item the union bound above ($2^d/(2d)$);
\item the Lov\'asz local lemma, with dependency degree at most
  $2^d\prod(a_i-1)$ \cite[Thm.~2]{CFP}, again $2^d/(2d)$;
\item the layer count of Proposition~\ref{prop:packing}: $n$ slices each using $\nu$
  features out of $F=2\binom{n}{2}^{d-1}$ satisfy the birthday-type condition
  $n\nu^2\lesssim F$, which for random-quality slices gives $n^{2d-1}\lesssim2^{2^d}$,
  i.e.\ $2^d/(2d-1)$;
\item the Zarankiewicz argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} in general dimension, which
  asks for the largest box-free \emph{subset} of $[n]^d$ of density $1/c^2$ and again
  gives $2^d/d$ in the exponent.
\end{enumerate}
Routes (c) and (d) indicate exactly what would be needed: a family of colourings (or of
box-free subsets) whose number of monochromatic boxes is a factor $2^{\Theta(2^d)}$
below the random count. Direct measurement suggests this does not happen: minimising the
number of monochromatic boxes over 2-colourings of $[n]^m$ by local search gives
$255$ versus a random expectation of $756$ at $(m,n)=(2,11)$, $424$ versus $1089$ at
$(2,12)$, and $65$ versus $364$ at $(3,9)$ --- ratios $0.34,0.39,0.18$, decreasing slowly
in $m$ but far from exponential. We therefore conjecture that the factor $d$ in the
exponent is not an artefact of the known arguments, and that a uniform $c$ requires a
different idea.

\section{Discussion and open problems}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \textbf{Exact values.} Not a single $\Ncd[3]$ with $c\ge2$ is known. The most
  accessible target is $N(2,3)\in[13,21]$. On the lower side one needs a box-free
  2-colouring of some $[n]^3$ with $13\le n\le20$. On the upper side Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2}
  shows that the binding quantity is $\varphi_2(n)$, the least number of monochromatic
  rectangles in a 2-colouring of $[n]^2$: by \eqref{eq:pack} any improvement of the lower
  bound $\varphi_2(n)\ge\mu(T_0,\binom{n}{2})+\mu(T_1,\binom{n}{2})$ that pushes
  $n\varphi_2(n)$ past $2\binom{n}{2}^2$ for some smaller $n$ immediately lowers
  $N(2,3)$. Our convexity bound is loose by roughly a factor of two already at $n=5$
  ($1$ against the exact value $2$, which we verified by exhaustive enumeration of column
  multisets), so there is room. The \emph{threshold} version of $\varphi_2$ is classical:
  $\varphi_2(n)=0$ for $n\le4$ and $\varphi_2(n)\ge1$ for $n\ge5$, the latter being the
  bipartite Ramsey number $r(C_4,C_4)=5$. What is not, to our knowledge, tabulated is the
  multiplicity $\varphi_2(n)$ itself for $n\ge6$. Determining it exactly is a
  self-contained two-dimensional problem --- the bipartite analogue of the Ramsey
  multiplicity problem --- and it is the quantity that Theorem~\ref{thm:upper2} makes
  binding.
\item \textbf{Beyond the shift family.} Proposition~\ref{prop:1dshift} and
  Section~\ref{sec:1dsearch} reduce the three-dimensional problem, for colourings constant
  on the level sets of $x+y$, to a two-dimensional corner condition. The natural next step
  is the family of colourings constant on the level sets of a general linear form,
  $\chi(x,y,z)=f(\alpha x+\beta y,z)$, or of two independent forms; each such choice
  yields its own clause set and its own notion of forbidden configuration, and we have not
  explored whether one of them is large enough to contain a colouring with $n\ge13$.
\item \textbf{Permutation families.} Replacing the cyclic shifts in \eqref{eq:shift} by
  arbitrary per-slice permutations $\pi_x$ of $[n]$ gives a family to which the orbit
  argument does not apply. A pigeonhole estimate shows that a random permutation family
  fails (for $n=12$ the expected number of collisions per constraint is about $2.7$), so
  a successful design would have to keep $|S(\gamma,\{z_1,z_2\})|\le3$ throughout; this is
  a $\lambda\le3$ design constraint and is, in our view, the most promising route to
  $n=12$ or beyond.
\item \textbf{The exponent gap.} Whether $N(2,d)\ge2^{c\,2^d}$ holds for a fixed $c>0$ is
  open. Sections~\ref{sec:growth} isolates the equivalent box-multiplicity question.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Colourings}\label{app:colourings}

We record our best construction for $c=2$, i.e.\ a box-free 2-colouring of $[11]^3$,
together with the rule that generates it. All colourings, for every $(c,n)$ of
Table~\ref{tab:construct}, are stored in the accompanying repository as JSON arrays
\texttt{grid[x][y][z]} (zero-based).

\paragraph{The $c=2$, $n=11$ colouring.} Let $Q=\{1,3,4,5,9\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{11}$ be
the set of non-zero quadratic residues modulo $11$ (equivalently, the base block of the
$(11,5,2)$ symmetric difference set), and for $y\in\{0,\dots,10\}$ put
$T_y=(Q+y)\bmod 11$. Define
\[
f(x,y,z)=1\iff (z-x)\bmod 11\in T_y .
\]
Explicitly,
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
$T_0=\{0,1,2,4,5,7\}$ & $T_1=\{0,1,3,5,6,10\}$ \\
$T_2=\{0,2,3,9,10\}$ & $T_3=\{0,3,5,7,8,9\}$ \\
$T_4=\{0,4,6,8,9\}$ & $T_5=\{1,2,3,6,8\}$ \\
$T_6=\{1,4,5,8,9,10\}$ & $T_7=\{1,6,7,9,10\}$ \\
$T_8=\{2,3,4,5,6,9\}$ & $T_9=\{2,5,6,7,8,10\}$ \\
$T_{10}=\{3,4,7,8,10\}$ & \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Why it works: for $y_1\neq y_2$ the translate structure gives
$|T_{y_1}\cap T_{y_2}|=2$ whenever $y_1-y_2$ is a non-zero residue and $|T_{y_1}\cap
T_{y_2}|=0$ when $y_1-y_2$ is a non-residue, the two common columns always differing by
$\pm2$; the complementary intersections are translates of $Q$ itself. Each pair of rows
therefore contributes one difference class only, which is precisely the Sidon-type
condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:sidon}, so the colouring is box-free. An exhaustive
check of all $\binom{11}{2}^3=166\,375$ boxes confirms this.

with $n\in\{11,14,16,18,20,22,24,28,32,36,40\}$, and for $c=5$ with
